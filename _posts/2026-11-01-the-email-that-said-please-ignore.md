Most of us have a folder full of emails we ignored. Newsletters. "Your cart misses you." Offers on credit cards we already have.

I have one ignored email that paid me **₹15,000**. This is its story.

## Act 1: The breakup

In August 2021 I joined a live online course on UI5 and OData at an SAP training institute. The fee was ₹20,000, in two instalments of ₹10,000. I paid the first one and logged in, full of the motivation every learner has in week one.

A few weeks later, the second instalment came due, along with a reminder email that contained the most generous line in the history of payment reminders:

> "Please ignore this email if you do not wish to continue with the course..."

Reader, I followed instructions. I ignored it.

The institute revoked my access the same day. No drama, no "we need to talk", no bill. Just a quiet, mutual, very adult breakup. For **four years**, we didn't speak. I assumed we'd both moved on.

## Act 2: The ex texts back

Fast forward to December 2025. I wanted to learn SAP CAP on BTP, and who should have exactly that course? My ex.

People change, I told myself. It's been four years. I paid the full **₹25,000** online, feeling very mature about the whole thing.

Then I waited for the course access.

And waited.

What arrived instead was an invoice from the past. Apparently I had an outstanding **"due" of ₹10,000** from the 2021 course, and they had helpfully adjusted my new payment against it. No new course until I cleared my dues.

I did what any reasonable person would do: I forwarded them their own email from 2021. *"Please ignore this email if you do not wish to continue."* Your words, not mine.

They were not impressed by their own words.

When I pushed back, the replies escalated. I would be receiving **summons from a court in Delhi**. And they could get my **bank account frozen**.

Over ₹10,000. For a course they had let me leave. Four years ago. By ignoring an email. As instructed.

> A training institute cannot freeze your bank account. Only a court or a competent authority can order that, after due process. If someone threatens it over email, it's a pressure tactic, not a legal step.
{: .callout .warning}

## Act 3: Plot armour (a.k.a. paperwork)

I could have panicked and paid. I could have written a furious review in all caps. Instead, I chose the most powerful weapon available to the Indian middle class: **process**.

**Step 1: Called in the bank.** Three days after paying, I raised a dispute on the transaction. The bank put the ₹25,000 **on hold**. The money was now in a sort of financial waiting room: not mine, but not theirs either. The bank sent the institute questions. The institute, consistent with its 2021 policy, ignored them.

**Step 2: The National Consumer Helpline.** NCH is the government's free, pre-litigation helpline. You file a grievance, they nudge the company, and many disputes end right there. Mine did not: the institute wouldn't agree to a full refund, so NCH closed the grievance in January 2026 and politely pointed me towards the Consumer Commission. Which, honestly, felt like being handed a sword.

**Step 3: The Consumer Commission.** In February 2026 I filed a complaint under the Consumer Protection Act, 2019, at the District Commission in my home district. The institute is in another state, but the Act lets a consumer file where they live. Home ground.

The complaint came with a full supporting cast:

- a synopsis and a list of dates and events (four years, neatly tabulated);
- the complaint itself, with a notarised affidavit;
- six annexures: both payment receipts, **the legendary "please ignore" email**, the December 2025 replies with the summons and account-freeze threats, the NCH closure report, and the bank's confirmation of the hold.

My asks: the full ₹25,000 back with 12% interest, ₹50,000 for the harassment, ₹5,000 for costs, and a formal declaration that the 2021 "due" was null and void. Court fee for all this? **Zero.** Claims under ₹5 lakh don't need one.

## Act 4: The twist nobody saw coming (except my annexures)

While the case was pending, the institute reached out with a new kind of message. Not summons. Not a frozen bank account. A **settlement offer**.

We settled out of court for **₹40,000 in full and final settlement**.

Let's do the maths:

| The maths | |
|---|---|
| Paid for the 2025 course | ₹25,000 |
| Received in settlement | ₹40,000 |
| Course access received | 0 days |
| **Net result of ignoring one email** | **+₹15,000** |

In May 2026 I filed a memo withdrawing the complaint as satisfied, and the Commission closed the case. Somewhere, a "please ignore" email is very proud of itself.

## Lessons from my extremely niche life experience

1. **Never delete emails.** Ignore them, archive them, give them the silent treatment. But keep them. My entire case stood on one sentence in a four-year-old reminder.
2. **Real summons don't come from the accounts team.** They come from a court. An email threatening them is not the same thing, so don't panic and pay.
3. **Dispute the payment early.** Once the bank put the money on hold, the other side lost its easiest win. Funny how that works: the only account that got "frozen" in this story was the payment to them.
4. **Go to NCH first.** It's free and quick. And even if it doesn't resolve things, the closure report becomes evidence that you tried to settle it nicely.
5. **The consumer forum is built for people like us.** You can file yourself, and claims up to ₹5 lakh need no court fee. A clear timeline, calm facts and good annexures do the heavy lifting.
6. **Ask for something specific.** Itemise what you want and attach proof for each part. "I'm upset" is a feeling; "₹25,000 plus interest" is a prayer the Commission can act on.

> **Useful starting points:** the National Consumer Helpline (call **1915** or use its website), and **e-Jagriti**, the government's portal for filing consumer complaints online.
{: .callout .tip}

So the next time a company tells you to "please ignore this email", go ahead and ignore it.

Just don't delete it.
